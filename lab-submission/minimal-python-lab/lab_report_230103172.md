### Objective

The objective was to investigate the effect of memory spacing on parallel performance and analyze scalability using the Karp-Flatt metric.

### Method

Shared memory was used by multiple processes. Different memory strides were tested to observe the impact of memory access patterns.

The tested strides were 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 elements. The process counts were 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16.

The results suggest that memory spacing can influence performance. However, the measurements do not show a clear and consistent performance boundary at exactly 64 bytes. Therefore, the results should not be treated as a direct measurement of the CPU cache-line size.

### 3.2 Process Scaling and Karp-Flatt Analysis

The Karp-Flatt metric was used to estimate the effective serial fraction of the parallel execution.

The formula is:

`e = (1/S - 1/p) / (1 - 1/p)`

where:

- `S` is the measured speedup.
- `p` is the number of processes.
- `e` is the effective serial fraction.

#### Stride 1

|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2643.250 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.00000 |
| 2 | 1522.980 | 1.736 | 0.868 | 0.15235 |
| 4 | 966.942 | 2.734 | 0.683 | 0.15442 |
| 8 | 697.338 | 3.790 | 0.474 | 0.15865 |
| 16 | 638.766 | 4.138 | 0.259 | 0.19110 |

#### Stride 16

| Processes | Execution time (ms) | Speedup | Efficiency | Karp-Flatt e |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2542.173 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.00000 |
| 2 | 1413.417 | 1.799 | 0.899 | 0.11198 |

| 8 | 610.410 | 4.165 | 0.521 | 0.13156 |
| 16 | 601.480 | 4.227 | 0.264 | 0.18571 |

### Analysis

Increasing the number of processes improved execution time, but the improvement was not linear.

At 16 processes, stride 16 achieved a speedup of 4.227 compared with 4.138 for stride 1.

Parallel efficiency decreased as the process count increased. The Karp-Flatt values also increased at higher process counts, indicating the growing influence of overhead and other non-parallel effects.

The stride 16 configuration performed better than stride 1 in the process-scaling experiment, especially at 2, 4, and 8 processes.

## 6. Phase 4 — Skewed Workloads and Dynamic Chunk Queue

### Objective

The objective was to compare static and dynamic scheduling strategies under an uneven workload.

### Method

The experiment used 8,000 work items and 8 processes.

The workload was intentionally skewed so that some items required more computation than others.

Three scheduling approaches were compared:

1. Contiguous block static scheduling.
2. Cyclic round-robin static scheduling.
3. Dynamic queue scheduling with chunk sizes 1, 8, 64, and 512.

Each strategy was measured three times. The median wall-clock time and load imbalance factor were recorded.

The load imbalance factor is represented by lambda (λ). A value closer to 1 indicates more balanced worker activity.

### 6.1 Scheduling Results

| Strategy | Median wall time (s) | Lambda |
|---|---:|---:|
| Contiguous Block Static | 7.047 | 2.548 |
| Cyclic Round-Robin Static | 3.626 | 1.109 |
| Dynamic Queue C=1 | 3.248 | 1.007 |
| Dynamic Queue C=8 | 3.485 | 1.011 |
| Dynamic Queue C=64 | 3.540 | 1.079 |
| Dynamic Queue C=512 | 4.690 | 1.514 |

### 6.2 Analysis

Contiguous block scheduling produced the worst result. Its load imbalance factor was 2.548 because different blocks contained significantly different amounts of work.

Cyclic round-robin scheduling improved the balance by distributing items more evenly among processes. Its execution time decreased to 3.626 seconds.

Dynamic scheduling with chunk size 1 achieved the best result:

- Median wall time: 3.248 seconds.
- Load imbalance factor: 1.007.
- Best dynamic chunk size: C = 1.

Compared with contiguous block scheduling, the best dynamic configuration achieved a speedup of 2.170×.

Compared with cyclic scheduling, it achieved a speedup of 1.116×.

Larger chunk sizes reduced the frequency of queue operations but increased the risk of uneven work distribution. The C=512 configuration was substantially slower than C=1.

### Conclusion

For this skewed workload, dynamic scheduling with a chunk size of 1 provided the best balance between workers and the lowest execution time.

The result demonstrates that dynamic scheduling can be useful when the computational cost of individual tasks is not uniform.

## 7. Overall Conclusions

The five phases investigated hardware telemetry, memory behavior, parallel scalability, computational throughput, matrix multiplication, and workload scheduling.

The main findings are:

1. **Hardware telemetry:** The average timer call latency was approximately 68.35 ns, providing a baseline for interpreting short benchmark measurements.

2. **False sharing:** Memory stride affected measured performance. Larger spacing performed better in some tests, but the experiment did not establish a definitive cache-line boundary.

3. **Parallel scaling:** Increasing the process count generally improved strong-scaling performance, but the speedup was not linear. The measured maximum speedup was 11.029 at 15 processes.

4. **Weak scaling:** Efficiency decreased as the total workload and process count increased, showing the impact of parallel overhead.

5. **Roofline and GEMM:** The naive matrix multiplication implementation was faster than the tested optimized variants. Block size 64 was the best blocked configuration.

6. **Dynamic scheduling:** Dynamic queue scheduling with C=1 achieved the best result for the skewed workload, with a wall time of 3.248 seconds and a load imbalance factor of 1.007.

The experiments also demonstrate the importance of repeated measurements, median values, IQR, and careful interpretation of benchmark results. The fastest theoretical approach is not necessarily the fastest implementation in practice.
